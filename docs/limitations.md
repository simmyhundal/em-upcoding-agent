# Limitations

## Limitations
- **Medicare-style claims, not a particular payer's.** The claim shape (CPT E/M codes 99211 to 99215, diagnoses, one row per visit) and the way the code mix is calibrated come from the public Medicare fee-for-service file, and the record-request rules follow Medicare provider (a records request, a deadline, signature rules, extrapolating from a random sample). Medicare Advantage plans adjudicate their members' claims themselves, under the same CPT and E/M documentation rules, so the detection logic carries over; their record requests, appeals and recovery terms follow plan contracts and CMS Medicare Advantage rules rather than the fee-for-service process modeled here. Commercial plans use the same E/M levels too, but differ more: a younger and healthier patient mix (so the expected 99215 rate and the screen's baseline change), their own provider contracts and fee schedules, and different record-request and recovery terms. Treat this as a test of method on one payer's shape of data, not as calibrated to a commercial book.
- **The dollar score is a ranking, not a forecast (release 5).** Net dollars use the optimistic recovery model (a sampled 99215 whose note shows any cloning sign is denied in full), providers have about 240 claims a year in the simulation, and $2,000 per honest provider accused is an assumption ([release 5 wrap-up](release5_wrapup.md)).
- **Version 1 only: the inputs were unrealistic and the AI saw records a payer would not have yet.** A payer normally screens on claims data alone. Medical records (the equivalent of this project's `documentation.csv`) reach a payer only after it requests them from the fee-for-service primary care physician, and that request usually comes after a provider is already suspected. Here, a records sample is handed to the AI *before* it decides. It stands in for a first records request, but the order of events is not how it works in provider.
- **Why that matters.** The AI's filtering power comes largely from that sample. With it the AI accused 0 of 17 honest providers; without it (the chat-session runs) it accused 14 of 17. So read the results as what the AI can do once records are in hand, not as detection from claims alone. Put another way, in version 1 the AI turns a records sample into a cited, checkable case file, and it is also the filter that decides which flagged providers move on, but its accuracy rests on that sample.
- **Fixed in versions 2 and 3.** [Milestone 5](https://github.com/simmyhundal/em-upcoding-agent/milestone/5) rebuilt the inputs the way a payer would receive them and models records requests as a later step. The "Does it work?" results are version 1; versions 2 and 3 are in the sections below and in the Eval table.
- **The documentation signal is cleaner than real records.** In this data the documented level is a noisy copy of the hidden truth, and the documented minutes are filled in to match it exactly, so time never disagrees with the decision-making level. Real records are messier: upcoding providers may also inflate the documentation, and time and decision making are recorded independently. Version 2 addresses this (separate MDM elements, independent time, cloned notes); it is used for the version 2 and version 3 results.
- **Other limits.** Synthetic data from one generator; two seeds; the usefulness score comes from an AI grader and is below its target; the citation check confirms cited visits exist, not that each one supports its sentence.
