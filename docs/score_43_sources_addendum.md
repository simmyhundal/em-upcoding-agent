# Score 4.3 sources, addendum: collection and cost for claims-data cases

[Back to the sources](score_43_sources.md) · [the proposal](score_43_proposal.md) · [volume screen](concepts/volume-screen.md) · issue 200

**What this page is.** A second, targeted search, done 2026-10-27, for the collection rate, cost, fees, timing and dispute effects that apply to the **claims-data lane**. In that lane a plan finds a provider from claims alone (for example impossible visits per day), requests records for a random sample, treats claims with no record as overpaid, extrapolates, and demands or opens an SIU case. The first pass ([sources, section 2](score_43_sources.md)) had one sourced rate, about 24% (Medicare benefit-integrity contractors), and nothing commercial. This pass looked for vendor, consultant, state and managed-care figures. Read-only research: web search and primary documents (PDFs read in full where they loaded). Every figure below gives its URL, publisher, year and what it measures.

## Summary

**The best-matched sources agree, and they explain themselves.** Every source that follows fraud-type, payer-initiated overpayments to cash lands at about **16% to 24% collected**. Four series point that way:

- **Medicare program-integrity contractor referrals, FY2014 cohort: 20% overall and 20% for Part B** (practitioner claims). This is the only true cohort figure: amounts referred in one year, followed 12 to 24 months.
- **Medicare benefit-integrity contractors, 2012 to 2013: 24%.**
- **Medicaid managed-care plans' own SIUs, 2015: 22%** of fraud- and abuse-related overpayments they identified. This is a health plan, not a government contractor, and is the closest setting found.
- **CMS's own adjustment factor for overpayments found by its predictive-analytics Fraud Prevention System: about 16%.** A modeled expectation, not measured cash.

**The same plans collected far more on ordinary overpayments.** The 38 Medicaid plans that collected 22% on fraud and abuse collected **68% of non-fraud overpayments** (billing errors) in the same year. In a Vermont Medicaid SIU audit, cases with a records request and denial of undocumented claims collected about **91%, with 8% more still being collected**, wherever management did not reverse the finding.

**Why the rates differ: the sources name the same two causes every time.**
1. **The provider stops billing the payer** (revoked, terminated, bankrupt, gone). The payer can then no longer offset the overpayment against new claims, and collection by demand letter mostly fails.
2. **The case is handed to law enforcement or the state.** Collection is barred or frozen until that case ends.

Fraud versus error is not itself the driver. Whether offset stays available, and whether the case stays with the plan, is.

**For the claims-data lane** (visits that did not happen, found from claims):

- **Lower than 25%: no source supports it as a default.** The 16% figure is a model input and the 7% figure is an older cohort (2007 referrals).
- **About 25%: supported, if the lane's providers behave like fraud referrals.** That means they exit the network or the case goes to law enforcement.
- **About 60% to 70%: supported, if the plan recoups by offset from a provider who keeps billing.** Sources: Medicaid plans' 68% on non-fraud overpayments; Vermont's roughly 91% where findings stood.
- **85%: no source supports it for this lane.**

**Commercial evidence is still missing.** No commercial payer, vendor, consultant or regulator source was found that publishes identified (or demanded) against collected dollars for provider overpayments. Vendor material gives fee rates and marketing case studies, not cohort collection rates; see "Searched, nothing usable" below.

**Do not stack an appeal reduction on these rates.** The 20%, 22% and 24% figures count dollars still in appeal or overturned as not collected; they are already net of appeals and time. A referral-lane collection rate taken from them needs no separate 15% appeal cut. Otherwise the result is 0.85 x 0.25, about 21%, and appeals are counted twice.

## Cost per case or per dollar recovered

- **Per case: no new source found.** The first pass's $3,459 (NHCAA 2013, $2.66M SIU budget over 769 closed investigations) and the $4,251 bottom-up remain the anchors. NHCAA survey results after 2013 are members-only. NY DFS collects total SIU cost in its annual SIU filing, but the filings are not published.
- **Per dollar collected:**
  - Medicare RAC FY2021: contingency fee 16.4% plus CMS administration 8.7% = **25% of gross collections**, and only 52% of gross collections reached the Trust Fund after overturns, fees and costs (row 6).
  - ClarisHealth's assumed internal cost of recovery is **6% of recoveries** (vendor blog).
  - NHCAA 2013's "10.2 to 1" return counts savings plus recoveries over budget, so it is not a cost per collected dollar.
- **Recommended:** keep the proposal's **$4,000 total per referral, range $2,000 to $15,000**. No new evidence moves it.

**Reading.** The time that matters is the delay before offset starts. A provider who is still billing is collected in about 2 to 3 months. Collection from one who has stopped, or whose case was handed out, takes 1 to 2 years or never happens. The score does not discount, so time matters only through this exit effect.

## Disputes and appeals

- **Already-netted rates.** Rows 1 to 3 count amounts in appeal or overturned as not collected. Use them without a separate appeal cut.
- **Extrapolated demands can collapse.** In OIG's FPS example (row 10), an E/M estimate made by applying a small review's 65% denial rate to three years of payments fell from $630K to about $4K (1%) once it was worked into overpayment determinations. One appeal cut $3.8M of $5.5M (69%). Law-firm marketing pages report commercial extrapolated demands cut by 50% to 96% (Frier Levitt, https://www.frierlevitt.com/articles/frier-levitt-successfully-reduces-multiple-overpayment-demands-for-three-medical-practices/ ; Polsinelli, https://www.polsinelli.com/hunter-a-morgan/matters-2). These figures come from search-snippet text, not pages I read; they are self-selected wins, not rates.
- **Internal reversals count too.** Vermont lost 44% of identified dollars when agency leadership granted three of four appeals by changing a rule retroactively (row 5). For a plan, the comparable risk is a provider-relations or medical-director override.
- **For visits with no record at all** (the lane's main finding), there is little for a provider to appeal on the merits; the dispute moves to the sample and the extrapolation. **No source measures appeal outcomes for "no record" findings separately.**
- **Commercial appeal and overturn rates for post-payment audit findings: no source found.** The first pass's 15% (Medicare RAC Level 1 overturned dollars over collected) stays the only dollar anchor, for ordinary demands.

## Open questions

1. **Does the simulation let a claims-data-lane provider keep billing after the demand?** That decides which branch applies, and it is the largest single lever (25% against 65%).
2. **Commercial cohort data.** Vendors track a 3- and 12-month "conversion rate" but do not publish it. A payer or vendor contact, or the members-only NHCAA survey, is the likely route.
3. **What share of impossible-volume providers leave the network** or are referred out within a year? No source found. OIG's 5% termination of suspected providers (row 3) is a floor for Medicaid plans.
4. **Appeals on "no record" findings specifically:** no source separates them from coding-level disputes.
5. **Cost per case:** NY DFS collects SIU cost and caseload in its filings but does not publish them; a public-records request could yield a commercial cost per case.
6. **The 2016 Medicare RAC fee rates:** AHA and Hedgeye conflict; check against the CMS award documents if fees are used.

Sensitivity of the proposed score 4.3 (ten test seeds each): an appeal reduction of 40% (on ordinary demands) takes workflow v4.1 on data 4.0 from $4,603 to $160, and 60% to -$3,395; a chart-review cost of $50 per claim takes it to -$25,946 and the GAO figure of $158 to -$105,650 (score 4.0 already carries this option). The per-chart cost is the assumption that moves the scores most, and no source supports our $8.61.
